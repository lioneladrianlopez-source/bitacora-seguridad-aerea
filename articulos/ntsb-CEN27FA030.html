Relato oficial de NTSB

Texto original en inglés. La ficha en castellano presenta los datos estructurados del suceso.

On October 24, 2026, about 0822 mountain daylight time, a Piper PA-28-235 airplane, N8777W, was destroyed when it was involved in an accident near Gunnison, Colorado. The pilot and two passengers sustained fatal injuries. The airplane was operated as a Title 14 Code of Federal Regulations Part 91 personal flight. According to preliminary information, the pilot had originally planned to depart on October 23, 2026, on a visual flight rules flight from Rifle Garfield County Airport (RIL), Rifle, Colorado, to Gunnison-Crested Butte Regional Airport (GUC), Gunnison, Colorado. The pilot then changed the departure date to October 24, 2026, due to a personal event with the passengers. The pilot knew that on October 23, 2026, the weather would be “clear,” but for the flight on October 24, 2026, he wanted to depart “really early in the morning” because that is when he thought the weather would be “clear.” On October 24, 2026, at 0746, the flight departed RIF without a flight plan and there were no air traffic control services while en route to GUC. ADS-B data showed the airplane on a southeasterly flight path from RIL as it flew about 1 nautical mile (nm) west of the Anthracite Range over an elevation of approximately 10,000 ft mean sea level (msl) (see figure 1). The airplane then made an approximate 270o left turn toward the west over an elevation that was approximately 9,500 ft msl followed by a right turn toward the northwest.

Figure 1. ADS-B flight track of the airplane. A witness who was hunting in the area stated he was at 9,300 to 9,500 ft msl when the airplane flew in a northerly direction and sounded “really low.” He said the airplane was about 100 feet agl and about 300 to 400 ft from his location, and there “was no way [the airplane] was going to clear the Anthracites.” He said the visibility was “not great,” and he thought to himself why was the pilot flying today. He heard the airplane come over the valley and perform a “couple of loops,” and did not know if the pilot was “poking” in the basins to find a way over the range. He said the mountain peaks were covered “pretty good” with clouds, but the clouds did not cover the treetops. A second witness, who was several hundred yards from the accident site, said he was looking at the mountain pass and saw the airplane fly over and behind him making a [turn]. The airplane appeared as if it was going to fly over the pass again, which had clouds and a little snow. As he watched the airplane, he could hear the airplane. He said the airplane appeared to “go straight down” and heard “the engine rev up” as it was descending. He said the airplane appeared to make a 180o turn and it was coming back when it went straight down. He said the airplane just barely went over the pass and within seconds it came out of the clouds and headed straight down. He stated that the entire time he heard or observed the airplane, it “sounded good.” The accident site was located about 18 nm northwest of GUC. Examination of the accident site revealed a wreckage path through trees oriented approximately 102o and an elevation of approximately 10,400 ft msl. All major components of the airplane were located at the accident site. The wings were separated from the fuselage that was consistent with impacting trees and terrain, and the fuselage had a tail to nose heading of about 137o. The engine was separated for the airframe, and the propeller blades displayed S-shaped twisting and bending. The airplane wreckage was retained from further examination. The terminal area forecast for GUC (airport elevation of 7,680 ft msl), issued at 0520 and valid on October 24, 2026, at 0600, reported: visibility – greater than 6 statute miles (sm), ceiling – 15,000 ft above ground level (agl). From 0700 to 1000, there would be temporary fluctuations in weather: visibility – 4 sm, light showers and rain, ceiling – broken at 5,000 ft agl. Weather observations for GUC were recorded as: At 0556, visibility - 10 sm, sky conditions – clear At 0656, visibility – 10 sm, sky conditions – few at 7,500 ft agl and scattered at 12,000 ft agl At 0756, visibility - 6 sm, moderate rain and mist, ceiling – broken at 3,200 ft agl and overcast at 4,400 ft agl According to pilot records, the pilot was issued a private pilot certificate with an airplane single-engine land rating on April 3, 2024. He received his most recent flight review on April 18, 2026, and at the time of the accident, he had accumulated a total flight time of about 278 hours.
